# From Bayou Questions to Cloud-Based Ecology: Progress from FacultyHack@Gateways 2026

**By Dr. Mary K. Beals**  
Assistant Professor of Practice, Biology  
Southern University and A&M College

This initial SGX3 blog draft reflects my progress during the virtual FacultyHack@Gateways 2026 training period, August 4-14, 2026, and will be expanded after the Gateways 2026 conference experience. At this stage, the work is still in development, but the direction is clear: I am redesigning ECOL 404B Ecology at Southern University and A&M College to help biology majors engage with ecology through course-based undergraduate research experiences, Science Gateway resources, and guided computational tools.

The course theme, **Where Bayous Meet Bytes**, captures the heart of the project. I want students to connect Louisiana ecosystems, wetlands, biodiversity, climate change, water quality, and coastal change with modern scientific tools that support data exploration and ecological reasoning. The goal is not to turn biology majors into computer scientists. The goal is to give them a realistic, supportive pathway into computational ecology so they can ask stronger questions, work with authentic datasets, visualize ecological patterns, and explain what those patterns mean.

During the FacultyHack training, I began refining the course from its previous fully asynchronous structure into a face-to-face Fall 2026 version that includes lecture, lab, and structured CURE work. This shift matters because students will now have more direct instructor support as they learn to use unfamiliar tools. The redesigned course will meet for lecture on MWF from 11:00-11:50 AM and lab on Friday from 1:00-3:50 PM in Fisher Hall 201. The face-to-face format gives me space to scaffold student learning more intentionally through mini-lessons, team check-ins, guided notebook walkthroughs, lab activities, and research milestones.

One of the most important decisions from the training was to keep the student-facing technology beginner-friendly. Instead of positioning advanced HPC as the first step, the course will use **NAIRR CloudBank Classroom** as the primary cloud workspace. Students will use CloudBank to store project files, access guided Jupyter notebook activities, and explore ecological datasets in a browser-based environment. This is a practical choice because it removes some of the technical barriers that can make computational work feel intimidating for students who are new to coding, data analysis, or cloud-based tools.

The supporting resources will include **Jupyter notebooks**, **Cornell Virtual Workshop**, and **HPC Carpentry**. Jupyter notebooks will allow students to combine data, code, visualizations, written interpretation, and reflection in one guided activity. Cornell Virtual Workshop will support onboarding and basic cloud-computing awareness. HPC Carpentry will help reinforce reproducible workflow habits, including file organization, documentation, and responsible use of computational tools. Together, these resources help keep the work accessible while still introducing students to the habits of modern ecological research.

The CURE structure is being built around the full research process. Students will form research teams, complete a team charter, develop ecological questions, work with Louisiana-focused datasets, create visualizations, interpret findings, and communicate their results during a **Digital Bayou Showcase**. The final deliverable will not simply be a report at the end of the semester. It will be the result of a staged process where students practice collaboration, data literacy, ecological reasoning, scientific communication, and reflection.

FacultyHack has also helped me think more carefully about sustainability. A course redesign is only useful if it can actually be implemented after the event. For that reason, I am developing both a full Fall 2026 syllabus and a short-form revised syllabus, along with a CURE module overview, student team charter, guided notebook activity, low-bandwidth backup activity, assessment rubric, and sample dataset. The low-bandwidth backup is especially important because access should not depend on perfect technology conditions.

This work is also connected to equity and opportunity. Southern University students deserve access to the same research-facing tools and computational experiences that are often associated with larger research-intensive institutions. By using Science Gateway resources in a structured, student-centered way, I can help students build confidence before they encounter more advanced computational tools in graduate school, internships, field research, or the workforce.

After the Gateways 2026 conference, I plan to update this blog post with what I learned from the poster session, conference conversations, and additional SGX3 community feedback. For now, the training has helped me move from a broad idea to a clearer implementation plan. The work is becoming more focused, more realistic, and more aligned with the students I serve. **Where Bayous Meet Bytes** is not just a course theme. It is a way to help students see Louisiana ecology as a living research space and themselves as capable emerging scientists who can investigate it.
